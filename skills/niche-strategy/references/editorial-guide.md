# Editorial guide

## Niche menu

Cover sea creatures, deep-sea life, prehistoric creatures, dinosaurs, land animals, fish and sharks, extinct animals, insects and arachnids, reptiles and amphibians, anatomy and extraordinary adaptations, and microscopic life and hidden ecosystems. Add one distinct recommendation. Differentiate overlapping niches by their subject or storytelling angle. Explain appeal without invented trend data or promises of virality.

## Ideas and reference style

Look for unusual anatomy, hidden abilities, survival strategies, scale comparisons, and scientific mysteries. Prefer a specific question with a payoff over a generic list. Borrow the reference's visual grammar: dark atmosphere, simple animated explanations, bold fact captions, anatomy reveals, size comparisons, and discoveries that build on each other. Adapt the setting to the subject. If a supplied video is inaccessible, state that and work from the available style description. Use original wording and scenes.

## Narration and sources

Open with a vivid fact, question, comparison, or imagined encounter. Advance the central question with each sentence and end on a reveal, callback, or memorable line. Use short spoken sentences, concrete comparisons, and accessible language. Avoid filler, generic introductions, unsolicited calls to subscribe, and exaggerated danger. Develop a fuller story for a longer runtime.

Aim for 130–150 spoken words per minute, or about 65–75 words for 30 seconds. Treat that as an estimate until the voiceover is read or measured. Keep timestamps, headings, delivery cues, and scene directions outside the narration.

Check central scientific claims against reliable sources, especially unusual behavior, measurements, superlatives, and extinct species. Separate observed facts from estimates and hypotheses. If verification is unavailable, say so and leave unsupported specifics out. Link sources in a short note after the narration.
